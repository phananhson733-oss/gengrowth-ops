## Ivory Westbrook: Discipline as the Only Thing Keeping Her Safe

Ivory isn't drawn as a naive ingenue — she's a pianist who has already learned that control is the only thing that keeps her safe. Her home life is described as difficult, and her training is demanding in a way that leaves no room for softness. That's an important detail, because it means she doesn't walk into Emeric's orbit looking for rescue. She walks in already fluent in discipline, obsession, and the idea that excellence has to be earned through suffering.

That backstory matters for how the romance plays out. A prodigy's path — the kind of training that produces someone capable of holding a professor's attention in the first place — usually means years of competitions, scholarships, and a career riding on staying flawless. Ivory isn't risking a casual crush; she's risking the one structure that's kept her stable. That's what makes her a much harder character to write off as "reckless" than a more conventional ingenue would be.

## Emeric Marceaux: The Professor Whose Control Is the Whole Point

Emeric is played as exacting and controlled on the surface — the kind of professor whose approval is rationed, not given freely. He's the authority figure who can make or break a student's trajectory with a single evaluation, and that authority is precisely what makes the pull between them dangerous rather than sweet.

Readers of Pam Godwin's original novel have described the dynamic as one where he shapes her playing with total authority, while she is the one who ultimately determines how far the relationship goes. That inversion — the student holding more real power than the title "professor" would suggest — is what keeps the forbidden-romance premise from feeling one-sided. It's not simply a man abusing a position of power; it's two people who each have something the other one needs, and neither is fully in control of where that leads.

## The Conservatory's Other Threats: Ivory's Brother, Emeric's Ex, and the Dean's Son

Dark Notes isn't a two-hander — the surrounding cast is doing real work to explain why Ivory and Emeric's situation is as pressured as it is. According to ReelShort's own character guides, Ivory's brother Shane Westbrook is written as abusive and struggling with drug and gambling addiction, which is the concrete shape behind the vague "difficult home life" line in the official synopsis — Ivory isn't fleeing mild family tension, she's fleeing a genuinely destructive one. On Emeric's side, Joanne is introduced as his seductive, deceiving ex, a built-in complication that suggests his past isn't as controlled as his classroom persona. And Prescott Rivard, the dean's son, is written as a bully who specifically targets Ivory at the conservatory — meaning she's contending with a hostile social environment even before the professor complicates things further.

Put together, these three characters answer a question the marketing copy leaves open: why does Ivory need Emeric to feel safe at all, given how dangerous that "safety" actually is? A hostile home, a resentful peer, and a professor's own unresolved past all raise the cost of walking away from him — which is a more mechanically interesting setup than "forbidden romance" alone implies.
