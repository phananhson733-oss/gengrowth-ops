What an internal link audit actually finds

Broken internal links

Links that 404 or chain through redirects. We show the source page, the broken target, and the anchor text used.

Find orphan pages nothing links to

Zero inbound internal links means search engines reach the page only via your sitemap — and treat it accordingly.

Orphans grouped by section, not one flat list

Grouped by URL pattern, so orphaned tag pages don't drown out orphaned product pages.

Pages with only one or two inbound links

Reachable only from a nav menu, never from real content. Usually the cheapest wins on the list.

Link equity distribution across your site

Your pages ranked by inbound internal links — so you can see which ones your structure has been quietly promoting.

Anchor text distribution

Surfaces "click here" anchors that say nothing, and identical anchors repeated across dozens of pages.

Crawl depth — how many clicks from home

Pages buried five clicks deep get crawled less and rank worse. Anything past three clicks is flagged.

Internal linking opportunities you already have

For each weak page, which of your existing pages already cover related ground — the natural places to link from.

Internal link visualization — the whole structure as a map

Clusters, isolated islands, and the pages everything routes through — structure a URL list can't show you.

How we check internal links and decide what's a problem

published thresholds

Published thresholds, not "AI-powered analysis." If you disagree with where we drew a line, at least you can see the line.

What we count as an orphan

A page in your sitemap that our crawl never reached by following internal links. That's it — no weighting, no score. If your sitemap is stale, the orphan list inherits that staleness, which is why we show the sitemap's last-modified date alongside the results.

What we count as "thin"

One or two inbound internal links, where those links come from navigation or footer rather than body content. We separate navigational from contextual links because search engines weight them differently — and because a page linked from every footer isn't really linked in any editorial sense.

Where the crawl starts and stops

We start from your homepage and your sitemap, follow internal links until we hit the page limit or run out of new URLs, and respect robots.txt. We don't execute long-running JavaScript, which is the main reason a crawl can miss links.

What this internal link audit won't tell you

3 known limits

Links that only exist after JavaScript runs

If your internal links are injected by client-side JavaScript, our crawler may not see them, and pages linked that way can show up as false orphans. If your site is a single-page app or heavily client-rendered, treat the orphan list as a starting hypothesis rather than a verdict.

Whether a link should exist at all

We can tell you a page has no inbound links. We can't tell you whether that page deserves to exist. Some orphans are orphaned because the content was thin, dated, or duplicated — and the honest fix is to remove or consolidate, not to add links. We flag candidates; the call is yours.

Wrong links, as opposed to broken ones

A link that works but points somewhere unhelpful — a CTA aimed at a tutorial when it should point at the tool — is invisible to any crawler, because technically nothing is broken. Detecting that reliably needs a map of what each page is supposed to link to. We're building toward it; today, this audit finds broken links, not misdirected ones.

Who this is for

Sites past roughly 50 pages, where you can no longer hold the link structure in your head. It matters most if content was produced in batches, if you've restructured URLs at some point, or if pages are generated from a template or feed.

Internal link audit vs. Screaming Frog

Screaming Frog is more thorough and we'd recommend it for a deep technical audit — it renders JavaScript, handles very large sites, and does far more than links. It's also a desktop application, capped at 500 URLs before you pay, and it hands you raw data to interpret yourself. This runs in a browser, needs no install, and returns a prioritized list rather than a spreadsheet. Different tools, different moments.

Internal link checker vs. Search Console's own report

Search Console shows internal link counts for pages it already knows about — which means orphan pages, the ones it struggles to find, are exactly the ones most likely to be missing or undercounted there. It also won't tell you which page to link from. This crawls independently of what Google has indexed.

Internal link audit FAQ

What is an internal link audit?
An internal link audit crawls your site the way a search engine does, records every link from one of your pages to another, and reports what that structure is doing: which pages are unreachable, which links are broken, and where authority is accumulating. It's structural — it looks at the connections between pages rather than at any single page's content.
How is this different from an internal link checker?
In practice the terms are used interchangeably. If there's a distinction, a checker verifies whether links work, and an audit also asks whether the structure those links create is doing what you want. This tool does both: it reports broken links, and it maps where your link equity ends up.
Do I need Search Console or site verification?
No. This crawls your public pages, which means no OAuth, no verification file, and no account. It also means you can audit a site you don't own — a competitor's, or one you're about to take over.
Does this find orphan pages too?
Yes. We compare the URLs in your sitemap against the URLs our crawl reached by following links. Anything in the sitemap we never reached is an orphan, and we group them by URL pattern so you can separate a genuine problem from auto-generated pages that were never meant to be linked.
How many pages will you crawl for free?
Up to [X] pages per crawl. If your site is larger, the free crawl covers your sitemap's most important pages first, and you can connect a project to crawl in full.
How often should I audit internal links?
For most sites, quarterly is enough, plus once after any structural change — a migration, a URL restructure, a template change, or a large batch of new content. The failure mode isn't gradual drift; it's a specific change that quietly disconnects a section, and that's worth checking for right after it happens.
Which broken links should I fix first?
Sort by the authority of the page the broken link sits on. A broken link on a page with 40 inbound links is wasting far more than the same link on a page nobody reaches. The exported list is ordered this way by default.
Should I fix every orphan page?
No. Ask first whether the page should exist. If it's thin, outdated, or duplicated elsewhere, removing or consolidating it is the better fix — adding links just spends authority on something that won't convert or rank. Rescue the orphans you'd have linked to anyway.
Will this work on a JavaScript-rendered site?
Partially. We don't execute long-running client-side JavaScript, so links injected that way may be missed and their targets can appear as false orphans. If your site is client-rendered, cross-check anything surprising before acting on it.
Can I export the results?
Yes, as CSV — the full link map, the orphan list, and the broken link list. If you're using GenGrowth as a project, the findings also carry into Step 3 (Site Structure & Internal Links) so you don't re-enter them by hand.
